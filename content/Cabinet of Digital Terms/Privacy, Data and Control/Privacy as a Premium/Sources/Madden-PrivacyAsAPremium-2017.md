## Madden et al. (2017) — Privacy, Poverty, and Big Data: A Matrix of Vulnerabilities for Poor Americans

**Madden, M., Gilman, M., Levy, K. & Marwick, A. (2017).** Privacy, Poverty, and Big Data: A Matrix of Vulnerabilities for Poor Americans. *Washington University Law Review*. https://openscholarship.wustl.edu/law_lawreview/vol95/iss1/6/

---

### What this source contributes

Original findings from a nationally representative US telephone survey with an oversample of low-income adults. Low-income internet users rely more on their phones: 63% of smartphone users in households earning under $20,000 a year mostly go online by phone, against 21% of those earning $100,000 or more. They also use privacy-protective strategies less often: 65% of low-income social media users have used privacy settings to limit who sees their posts, against 79% in wealthier households, and fewer feel they know enough to manage those settings (65% against 77%). The authors note that mobile access narrowed the digital divide but left low-income Americans open to new forms of tracking, and that poorer Americans are less likely to use the more strongly encrypted, more expensive phones. They then follow big data into employment screening, college admissions and predictive policing, and weigh privacy law, digital literacy and notice-and-choice against class-based vulnerability.

### Analytical function in Privacy as a Premium

The evidence for the entry's central claim: who ends up with the least privacy is not random but follows income. The survey predates pay-or-consent; it shows the inequality that a price on privacy builds on.
