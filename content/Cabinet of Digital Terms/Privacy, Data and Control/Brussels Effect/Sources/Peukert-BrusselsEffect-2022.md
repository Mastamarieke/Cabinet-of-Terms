## Peukert et al. (2022) — Regulatory Spillovers and Data Governance: Evidence from the GDPR

**Peukert, C., Bechtold, S., Batikas, M. & Kretschmer, T. (2022).** Regulatory Spillovers and Data Governance: Evidence from the GDPR. *Marketing Science*. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2021.1339 · open version: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3560392

---

### What this source contributes

The study follows more than 110,000 websites and their third-party requests for twelve months before and six months after the GDPR came into force. Websites substantially reduced their connections to outside web technology providers, and this held for websites the GDPR did not legally bind; the changes were strongest among less popular sites and for the collection of personal data. Market concentration in web technology rose: all vendors lost, but the largest, Google, lost relatively less and significantly increased its market share in advertising and analytics.

The authors offer two likely explanations, without claiming to prove them. Under the GDPR a website that shares data with a third party must inform its users and may be jointly liable, so sharing within one firm is less risky than sharing across many, and websites may choose large providers that "have more resources to weather legal challenges". And the consent requirement favours firms with many services: since most internet users already use some of Google's services, Google could gather consent from most of them, covering a broad range of its products; "the larger a service provider becomes, the cheaper it may become to gather broad user consent." They note that they cannot quantify the costs of the higher concentration.

### Analytical function in Brussels Effect

The empirical evidence for the effect and for its friction at once. It shows a European rule changing websites outside Europe, which is the Brussels Effect measured, and it shows the same rule strengthening the largest company in the market it was meant to restrain.
