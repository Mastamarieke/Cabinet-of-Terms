  In 2018 the GDPR, Europe's privacy law, came into force. Researchers followed more than 110,000 websites and saw them remove trackers, the pieces of code that tell advertising companies who is visiting, also on websites outside Europe that the law did not cover. The smaller tracking companies lost the most; Google lost the least and won market share. ***The charger of your phone now fits almost every other phone. Who made that happen?***

**Literal meaning:** *Brussels* is the city where the European Union has its institutions, and in the news its name stands for them. An *effect* is what follows from a cause. The **[[Brussels Effect]]** is the way EU rules become the world's rules without other countries having to agree to them. A company that wants to sell in Europe must follow European law, and it is often cheaper to make one version of a product for everyone than one for Europe and another for the rest.

**Origin:** The law professor Anu Bradford named it in 2012 ([[Bradford-BrusselsEffect-2012|Bradford, 2012]]: the article in the Northwestern University Law Review that coined the term) and expanded it in her book *The Brussels Effect* (2020). She showed EU rules on privacy, chemicals and food becoming global standards first because companies changed their products everywhere. ==Governments elsewhere sometimes followed with EU-style laws, for instance when those same companies lobbied for them== ([[Secondary-BrusselsEffect#11. The global reach of the EU's approach to digital transformation|Niestadt, 2024]]: European Parliament briefing on Bradford's two routes, through companies and through governments).

> The way EU rules spread across the world, because companies find it cheaper to apply one standard everywhere than to keep separate versions.

**The Appeal:** The **[[Brussels Effect]]** shows that one market with enough buyers can set rules for the largest technology companies. When the EU required USB-C on new phones, Apple's Greg Joswiak said ["Obviously, we'll have to comply; we have no choice"](https://www.macworld.com/article/1362886/apple-confirms-future-iphones-will-switch-from-lightning-to-usb-c.html "Read Apple's reaction (Simon, Macworld, 2022)."), and ==the iPhone 15 became the first iPhone with USB-C== ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightning_(connector)): the history of Apple's Lightning connector). Some of the rules make privacy the default: ==the GDPR==[^gdpr] turned [[Privacy by Design]] from a principle into a legal duty.

**The Friction:** Brussels did, through the **[[Brussels Effect]]**, and the same reach can also strengthen the companies it is meant to restrain. After the GDPR took effect in May 2018, ==websites cut their links to outside tracking firms, even sites the law did not bind, while Google lost least and gained market share== ([[Peukert-BrusselsEffect-2022|Peukert et al., 2022]]: more than 110,000 websites followed from a year before the GDPR to six months after).

The rules reach companies outside Europe, but enforcement does not always follow. ==The Dutch Data Protection Authority==[^ap] ==fined Clearview AI for a database of billions of faces taken from the internet, and expected it would not pay== ([NOS, 2024](https://nos.nl/artikel/2535633-boete-van-privacywaakhond-voor-verzamelaar-van-miljarden-foto-s-van-gezichten): €30.5 million in 2024, after an unpaid French fine), a case of [[Biometric Surveillance]] built on the logic of [[Surveillance Capitalism]]. The rules also reach a platform run by one man. ==The European Commission fined X for its deceptive blue checkmark and for shutting out researchers== ([Nicol-Schwarz & Kolodny, 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/12/05/elon-musks-x-fined-140-million-by-european-commission.html): €120 million under the Digital Services Act, December 2025), and what the fine meets is [[Muskism]], private infrastructure and personal authority without the accountability that usually comes with that much power. Meta answered the GDPR with a choice between paying for its apps and being tracked, ==and the Commission fined it for that choice== ([AP, 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/04/23/nx-s1-5373560/apple-meta-eu-tech-fines): €200 million under the Digital Markets Act, April 2025). That choice is [[Privacy as a Premium]], where not being tracked becomes something you pay for.

The reach stops where American law takes over, since the US [[CLOUD Act]] lets American authorities demand data from American cloud companies wherever it is stored. That collides with the GDPR for every European organisation on American servers, and [[Vendor Lock-in]] makes leaving those servers hard. Not every rule travels either, and ==only in the EU can iPhone users install apps from other app stores== ([Dotson, 2024](https://siliconangle.com/2024/03/06/apple-releases-ios-17-4-bringing-alternative-marketplaces-eu-upgrades/): since March 2024, under the Digital Markets Act). The rules also run through national regulators. Because [[Roblox]] has its European office in the Netherlands, the Dutch consumer authority investigates it for the whole EU. One question is whether [[Dark Patterns]], design that steers people into choices they did not mean to make, push children into buying [[Robux]], the game's own currency.

[^gdpr]: The General Data Protection Regulation: the EU's rules on how organisations may use personal data, and the rights people have over it. It has applied since 25 May 2018. [European Commission: 'Data protection in the EU'](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/data-protection-eu_en)
[^ap]: The Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP), the independent Dutch supervisor of how personal data is processed. [Wikipedia: 'Dutch Data Protection Authority'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Data_Protection_Authority)

**Why This Matters:** The **[[Brussels Effect]]** explains why a rule made in Brussels changes the phone and the apps you use, wherever you live, and why some of your rights travel with them. At a cookie banner it is easy to miss what you agree to: ==one click on the Daily Mail's website accepted the terms of 835 companies in 0.0146 seconds==, many of them advertisers and [[Data Brokers]], firms that trade personal data; the artist Julia Janssen printed them, and passers-by took turns reading them aloud ([De Ruiter, 2019](https://www.volkskrant.nl/nieuws-achtergrond/binnen-0-0146-seconden-wisten-835-bedrijven-wie-julia-was-ze-vatte-het-angstbeeld-in-een-kunstwerk~bbb8e6ec/): 826 pages at Dutch Design Week, although the GDPR requires such terms to be clear). Some of it can be undone, because [anyone in Europe can ask an organisation to delete their data, and it must answer within a month](https://autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/nl/zelf-doen/gebruik-uw-privacyrechten/recht-op-vergetelheid "Read how to ask for your data to be deleted (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens)."). Whether rules written in Brussels restrain the largest companies or mostly reshape them is still open. The next time a cookie banner or a new setting appears, ask whether a European rule put it there and what it lets you refuse; if the choice exists only in Europe, the effect stopped at the border.
