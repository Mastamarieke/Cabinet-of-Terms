***The EU passed a law. A company in California changed its global product.***

**Literal meaning:** The **Brussels Effect** describes the mechanism by which EU regulation becomes the effective global standard for a sector, not through international treaty or diplomatic pressure but through the commercial logic of market access: companies that want to operate in the EU market must comply with EU rules, and compliance is often more economical as a single global standard than as a market-by-market variation.

**Origin:** The concept was developed and named by the Columbia Law School professor Anu Bradford in [[Bradford-BrusselsEffect-2012|Bradford (2012)]], an article in the *Northwestern University Law Review*, and expanded in her book *The Brussels Effect* (2020). She showed EU rules on privacy, chemicals and food becoming global standards because companies changed their products everywhere, not because other governments copied them.

> The mechanism by which EU regulatory standards spread globally — because companies find it cheaper to apply one standard everywhere than to maintain separate versions.

**The Appeal:** The **Brussels Effect** demonstrates that effective regulation of global technology companies is possible from a single jurisdiction with sufficient market power. The charging port shows how it works: when the EU required USB-C on new phones, Apple's Greg Joswiak said, "Obviously, we'll have to comply; we have no choice" ([Simon, 2022](https://www.macworld.com/article/1362886/apple-confirms-future-iphones-will-switch-from-lightning-to-usb-c.html)), and the iPhone 15 (2023) became the first iPhone with USB-C ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightning_(connector))). For advocates of technology governance, it is the most practical available path.

**The Friction:** The **Brussels Effect** can also strengthen the companies it is meant to restrain. [[Peukert-BrusselsEffect-2022|Peukert et al. (2022)]] followed more than 110,000 websites from a year before the GDPR came into force to six months after: sites cut their connections to outside web technology firms, including sites the GDPR did not bind, but Google lost relatively less and gained market share in advertising and analytics.

The rules reach companies outside Europe, but enforcement does not always follow. In 2024 the Dutch Data Protection Authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million for a database of billions of faces taken from the internet, [[Biometric Surveillance]] built on [[Surveillance Capitalism]], and expected it would not pay, as it had never paid its French fine either ([NOS, 2024](https://nos.nl/artikel/2535633-boete-van-privacywaakhond-voor-verzamelaar-van-miljarden-foto-s-van-gezichten)). The rules also reach one owner's platform: in December 2025 the European Commission fined X €120 million under the Digital Services Act for its deceptive blue checkmark, its advertising archive and shutting out researchers ([Nicol-Schwarz & Kolodny, 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/12/05/elon-musks-x-fined-140-million-by-european-commission.html)). [[Muskism]] names what the fine meets: private infrastructure and personal authority without the accountability that normally comes with power at this scale. Meta answered the GDPR with a choice between paying for its apps and being tracked, and in April 2025 the Commission fined it 200 million euros for it under the Digital Markets Act ([AP, 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/04/23/nx-s1-5373560/apple-meta-eu-tech-fines)). That is [[Privacy as a Premium]]: not being tracked becomes something you pay for.

The reach has limits. The US [[CLOUD Act]] requires American cloud providers to hand data to American authorities on request, wherever the data is stored, which conflicts with the GDPR for every European organisation on American cloud infrastructure; [[Vendor Lock-in]] makes leaving that infrastructure hard. And not every European rule travels: since March 2024 the Digital Markets Act has let iPhone users in the EU, and only there, install apps from other app stores ([Dotson, 2024](https://siliconangle.com/2024/03/06/apple-releases-ios-17-4-bringing-alternative-marketplaces-eu-upgrades/)).

The Digital Services Act also runs through national regulators. Because the European office of [[Roblox]] is Dutch, the Autoriteit Consument & Markt decides for the whole EU market; in January 2026 it opened an investigation into harmful content, adults approaching minors, and [[Dark Patterns]], design that steers people into choices they did not mean to make, that may push children into buying [[Robux]], the game's own currency.

**Why This Matters:** **Brussels Effect** names the most consequential mechanism of global technology governance currently in operation. Once you see EU market access as a regulatory lever, you can read EU digital regulation as having global scope, and assess its adequacy accordingly.
