***Companies you have never heard of know where you live, what you earn, and what your health conditions are.***

**Literal meaning:** **Data brokers** are companies that collect personal information about individuals from a wide range of sources, such as public records, purchase histories, social media, location data, loyalty programmes and court records, aggregate it into detailed profiles, and sell access to those profiles to advertisers, insurers, employers, political campaigns, law enforcement, and other clients.

**Origin:** The data brokerage industry developed from direct mail marketing databases in the 1970s–80s, scaling with the internet and accelerating after the smartphone created continuous location and behavioural data streams. Major brokers including Acxiom, Experian, Equifax, LexisNexis, and Oracle Data Cloud maintain profiles on hundreds of millions of people. A 2014 FTC report *Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability* documented the industry's scale and opacity. Most consumers have no knowledge of which brokers hold their data or what it contains.

> Companies that assemble detailed personal profiles from aggregated data sources and sell them to anyone who pays — operating largely outside public awareness.

**The Appeal:** Data broker services have legitimate applications: fraud prevention, credit assessment, identity verification, and law enforcement. The aggregated data enables services that would otherwise require extensive individual disclosure.

**The Friction:** The people profiled rarely know. Their data reaches **data brokers** through routes they never see: a hack of Gravy Analytics in January 2025 showed locations taken from thousands of ordinary apps through their advertising systems, often without the app makers knowing ([Cox, 2025](https://www.404media.co/candy-crush-tinder-myfitnesspal-see-the-thousands-of-apps-hijacked-to-spy-on-your-location/)), and every time a European opens a site or app, data about what they read and where they are is sent out to companies bidding to show an advert, on average 376 times a day ([ICCL, 2022](https://www.iccl.ie/news/iccl-report-on-the-scale-of-real-time-bidding-data-broadcasts-in-the-u-s-and-europe/)). What is sold is often wrong. Through Facebook's partnership with brokers, [[Venkatadri-DataBrokers-2019|Venkatadri et al. (2019)]] found more than 90% of US accounts linked to broker data, and at least 40% of the attributes their volunteers checked were not at all accurate, financial information included. Those profiles still decide things: the advert or political message a person sees ([[Microtargeting]]), a name they stopped using that keeps coming back ([[Deadnaming]]), a score that is never explained ([[Algorithmic Violence]]), and a home address that anyone can buy: in 2013 a gunman who targeted a Florida judge had bought the judge's address online for $1.95 ([US Courts, 2020](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/statement_of_director_duff_dec_2020_final.pdf)), the raw material of [[Doxing]], publishing someone's private details to intimidate them. [[Surveillance Capitalism]] is the logic that makes the trade pay. Telling people more does not end it: [[Crain-DataBrokers-2018|Crain (2018)]] argues that transparency runs into the business itself, because personal information has become a commodity. European rules give rights of access and deletion, the [[Brussels Effect]], but brokers abroad use the gaps between countries.

**Why This Matters:** Once you see **data brokers**, "your data" stops meaning what you gave and starts meaning what others have put together about you. It shows in small moments: an advert that seems to know your income, a letter addressed to a name you no longer use. The question then is who holds a profile of you, and what is in it. Some places have started to answer: since August 2026 more than 600 registered brokers in California must process deletion requests sent through a single state platform ([Malwarebytes, 2026](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/08/californians-can-tell-data-brokers-to-drop-their-information)), and in Europe anyone can ask an organisation what it holds about them. Whether that reaches brokers who sell across borders is still open.
