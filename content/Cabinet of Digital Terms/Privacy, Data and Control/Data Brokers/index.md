***Companies you have never heard of know where you live, what you earn and what your health conditions are. How did they find out?***

**Literal meaning:** A *broker* is a go-between who arranges a sale between a buyer and a seller. The word goes back to Anglo-Norman *brocour*, a small trader. A broker usually works for two parties who both know the deal, but a [[Data Brokers|data broker]] trades in information about people who are neither buyer nor seller. **Data brokers** are companies that collect what is known about you from many places, such as public records, purchases, apps, location data and loyalty cards. They combine it into a profile and sell access to that profile to advertisers, insurers, employers, political campaigns, police and anyone else who pays.

> Companies that put together detailed personal profiles from many sources and sell them to anyone who pays, mostly without the people in them knowing.

**Origin:** The trade grew out of the address lists of direct mail in the 1970s and 1980s, and took off with the internet and the smartphone, which produce a constant stream of locations and behaviour. Large brokers such as Acxiom, Experian, Equifax and LexisNexis keep profiles on hundreds of millions of people, and ==most people do not know which brokers hold their data or what it says== ([[Secondary-DataBrokers#1. Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability|FTC, 2014]]: the US regulator's report on the industry's scale and opacity).

> Companies that put together detailed personal profiles from many sources and sell them to anyone who pays, mostly without the people in them knowing.

**The Appeal:** Data brokers have real uses, such as catching fraud, checking credit, confirming identity and helping the police. Combined data spares people from handing over the same details again and again.

**The Friction:** Along ==routes you never see==[^rtb]: your data reaches **[[Data Brokers|data brokers]]** without anyone asking you. ==A hack of Gravy Analytics showed locations taken from thousands of ordinary apps, often without the app makers knowing== ([Cox, 2025](https://www.404media.co/candy-crush-tinder-myfitnesspal-see-the-thousands-of-apps-hijacked-to-spy-on-your-location/): January 2025, through the apps' advertising systems).

[^rtb]: One of those routes is the advert auction. Every time you open a site or an app in Europe, what you read and where you are goes out to companies bidding to show you an advert, on average 376 times a day. [ICCL: 'Report on the scale of Real-Time Bidding data broadcasts in the U.S. and Europe'](https://www.iccl.ie/news/iccl-report-on-the-scale-of-real-time-bidding-data-broadcasts-in-the-u-s-and-europe/)

==At least 40% of what brokers had on volunteers was not at all accurate, financial information included== ([[Venkatadri-DataBrokers-2019|Venkatadri et al., 2019]]: Facebook linked more than 90% of US accounts to broker data, tested with 183 volunteers), and still the profiles decide things. They steer the advert or political message you see ([[Microtargeting]]). They bring back the birth name a trans or non-binary person no longer uses ([[Deadnaming]]), because brokers sell whatever they hold, old names included. ==They feed scores that decide on a loan, a job or a home without explaining why== ([[Secondary-DataBrokers#2. The Scored Society: Due Process for Automated Predictions|Citron & Pasquale, 2014]]: the scored society), a form of [[Algorithmic Violence]], harm done by automated systems in which the blame lands on the people nearest them, not on those who built them. They also sell home addresses. ==In 2013 a gunman who targeted a Florida judge had bought the judge's address online for $1.95== ([US Courts, 2020](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/statement_of_director_duff_dec_2020_final.pdf): statement by the director of the US courts), and such addresses are the raw material of [[Doxing]], publishing someone's address or phone number to intimidate or stalk them.

The trade pays because of [[Surveillance Capitalism]], the business of selling predictions made from what people do. Telling people more does not end it, as ==transparency runs into the business itself once personal information has become a commodity== ([[Crain-DataBrokers-2018|Crain, 2018]]: why disclosure cannot fix the trade). European rules that travel abroad, the [[Brussels Effect]], give rights of access and deletion, but brokers elsewhere use the gaps between countries.

**Why This Matters:** Knowing about **[[Data Brokers|data brokers]]** means knowing that "your data" includes what others have put together about you, not only what you gave. ==Since August 2026 more than 600 registered brokers in California must handle deletion requests sent through one state platform== ([Malwarebytes, 2026](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/08/californians-can-tell-data-brokers-to-drop-their-information)), and in Europe [anyone can ask an organisation what it holds about them](https://autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/nl/zelf-doen/gebruik-uw-privacyrechten/recht-op-inzage "Read how to ask an organisation what it holds about you (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens)."). Whether that reaches brokers who sell across borders is still open. The next time an advert seems to know your income, or post arrives for a name you no longer use, ask who holds a profile of you and what is in it.
