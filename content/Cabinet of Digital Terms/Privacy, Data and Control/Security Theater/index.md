***You took off your shoes at the airport security check and felt safer. Were you?***

**Literal meaning:** *Security* means being free from danger, from Latin *securus*, without care. A *theater* is a performance put on for an audience. In **[[Security Theater|security theater]]** the audience is the people who are supposed to be protected, and the performance is that they feel safe. The term names measures designed to be seen more than to stop anything. They calm people down and leave the risk where it was.

**Origin:** Bruce Schneier, a security technologist, coined the term in *Beyond Fear* (2003) for aviation security after 9/11. Passengers took off their shoes, took out their laptops and walked through body scanners. In his view these steps were highly visible and of little use against a determined attacker. Their main work was to make passengers feel protected and to let politicians be seen doing something.

> Security measures that are meant to be seen more than to protect, so that safety is performed without being provided.

**The Appeal:** After a failure, people want to see a response, and a visible measure gives them one whether or not it works. For an organisation, a measure it can point to protects it from blame and fines when something goes wrong.

**The Friction:** Often not: you do what the measure asks, and the risk stays where it was, because the measure was made to be seen. Since July 2025 British platforms have had to check that their users are adults, and ==within days gamers passed Discord's face scan with the face of a character from the video game Death Stranding== ([[Secondary-SecurityTheater#3. Death Stranding is helping UK users bypass age verification laws|Gizmodo, 2025]]: age checks under the Online Safety Act). You hand over your face, a piece of [[Biometric Surveillance]], for a lock that a game character can open. Schools buy face recognition for the same reason, and critics of it in American schools called it ==security theatre that offers only the appearance of safer schools== ([[Andrejevic-SecurityTheater-2020|Andrejevic & Selwyn, 2020]]: Learning, Media and Technology, on face recognition in schools). Passwords show how long such a rule can last. ==In 2003 Bill Burr wrote the American rule that a password needs capitals, digits and symbols and must be changed every ninety days== ([[Secondary-SecurityTheater#4. NIST's new password guidelines|Engadget, 2017]]: Burr worked at NIST, the US standards institute). People could not remember such passwords, so they used patterns, *Welcome01!* became *Welcome02!*, and their passwords became ==easy to guess==[^maga]. In 2017 Burr said he regretted the rules he had written. With personal data the same move is called [[Privacy Washing]], where reassurance takes the place of protection.

[^maga]: In October 2020 the Dutch security researcher Victor Gevers guessed the Twitter password of US president Donald Trump, "maga2020!", a word, a year and a symbol, on his fifth attempt; two-factor authentication was off. He reported it, and Dutch prosecutors did not charge him because he met the criteria for an ethical hacker. [[Secondary-SecurityTheater#5. Ethical Dutch Hacker Guessed Trump's Twitter Password Twice|Newsweek: 'Ethical Dutch Hacker Guessed Trump's Twitter Password Twice']]

**Why This Matters:** Knowing the term **[[Security Theater|security theater]]** helps you tell a check that protects you from one that only makes you feel protected, so that your trust, your time and your data go where they do something. Schneier argues that it is not harmless: it takes money and attention away from measures that work, and people who feel safe become less careful. ==NIST has since dropped Burr's rules: its guidelines of 2025 forbid demanding a mix of characters or regular changes, and ask for a long password instead== ([[Secondary-SecurityTheater#6. Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Authenticator Management (SP 800-63B-4)|NIST, 2025]]: Digital Identity Guidelines, SP 800-63B-4), which a study with more than 5,000 people had already shown to work: ==a password of at least sixteen characters was harder to guess than one full of symbols, and easier to remember== ([[Komanduri-SecurityTheater-2011|Komanduri et al., 2011]]: CHI, four password rules compared). Yet ==in 2022 nearly half of the 120 most popular websites still demanded certain characters== ([[Secondary-SecurityTheater#7. Password policies of most top websites fail to follow best practices|Lee et al., 2022]]: SOUPS, password policies of the top websites). The next time a check asks something of you, your shoes, your face or a symbol in your password, ask what it actually stops and who has tested it; if no one can tell you, you are probably watching theater.
