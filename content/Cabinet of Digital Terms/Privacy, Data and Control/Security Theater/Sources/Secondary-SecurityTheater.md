## 4. NIST's new password guidelines

**Author:** Engadget
**Year:** 2017
**Type:** Journalism
**Publisher:** Engadget, 8 August
**URL:** https://www.engadget.com/2017-08-08-nist-new-password-guidelines.html

### What this source contributes

Bill Burr, who wrote NIST's 2003 password guidance (capitals, digits and symbols, and changes every ninety days), said "Much of what I did I now regret" (first reported by The Wall Street Journal). People changed one character at a time, and the passwords became hard to remember and easy to guess.

## 5. Ethical Dutch Hacker Guessed Trump's Twitter Password Twice

**Author:** Nick Colarossi
**Year:** 2020
**Type:** Journalism
**Publisher:** Newsweek, 17 December
**URL:** https://www.newsweek.com/ethical-dutch-hacker-guessed-trumps-twitter-password-twice-1555676

### What this source contributes

On 16 October 2020 the Dutch security researcher Victor Gevers guessed Donald Trump's Twitter password, "maga2020!", on his fifth attempt; two-factor authentication was off. Dutch prosecutors did not charge him: "has met the criteria that have been developed in case law to go free as an ethical hacker."

## 6. Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Authenticator Management (SP 800-63B-4)

**Author:** National Institute of Standards and Technology
**Year:** 2025
**Type:** Government guidance
**Publisher:** NIST, July
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-63B-4

### What this source contributes

The current US guidelines: passwords used on their own must be at least 15 characters; verifiers "SHALL NOT impose other composition rules (e.g., requiring mixtures of different character types)" and "SHALL NOT require subscribers to change passwords periodically", only after evidence of compromise.

## 7. Password policies of most top websites fail to follow best practices

**Author:** Kevin Lee, Sten Sjöberg, Arvind Narayanan
**Year:** 2022
**Type:** Academic (peer-reviewed conference paper)
**Publisher:** SOUPS 2022 (USENIX)
**URL:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2022/presentation/lee

### What this source contributes

Of the 120 most popular websites, only 13% followed all relevant best practices; 75% did not stop the most common passwords such as "P@$$w0rd", and 45% required specific character classes "for minimal security benefit".
