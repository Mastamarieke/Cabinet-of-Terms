***The algorithm denied the benefit. No one decided. No one is responsible.***

**Literal meaning:** An *algorithm* is a fixed series of steps that solves a problem; the word comes from the name of the ninth-century Persian mathematician al-Khwārizmī. *Violence* usually means physical force that causes harm. **Algorithmic violence** joins the two: harm done by following the steps, without anyone raising a hand. It names the harm of automated decision-making systems, such as a benefit refused, a face misidentified or a fraud check aimed at the wrong people, where no single person decided and no one is held responsible.

**Origin:** The artist ==Mimi Onuoha==[^onuoha] proposed the term in 2018, in an essay she published as a work in progress: [the violence that an algorithm or automated decision-making system inflicts by preventing people from meeting their basic needs](https://github.com/MimiOnuoha/On-Algorithmic-Violence "Read Onuoha's essay, 'Notes on Algorithmic Violence' (2018)."). She built it on *structural violence*, Johan Galtung's term of 1969 for harm done by social structures rather than by a person. Long before, Donna Haraway had described how information systems carry old hierarchies on while looking neutral, the [[Informatics of Domination]]; in 2018 Virginia Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* and Safiya Umoja Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* documented it in welfare systems and search engines. Researchers then asked how to study it: ==how algorithmic systems feed violence, and how they make it look legitimate== ([[Bellanova-AlgorithmicViolence-2021|Bellanova et al., 2021]]: a collective discussion in international political sociology).

> Harm produced by automated systems whose design encodes existing inequalities — where no individual decides, and no individual is accountable.

**The Appeal:** A computer, the reasoning goes, cannot discriminate: it only looks at the numbers, the belief that [[Dataism]] names. Automated systems promise the same rules for everyone, with no tired official, no favourites and no bad day, and for governments that handle millions of benefit claims or student grants that consistency and speed are real advantages. The same promise appeals to those who want to remove people at scale: in April 2025 the acting director of ==ICE==[^ice] said deportation should run [["Like Prime, but with human beings."|"like Prime, but with human beings"]] ([[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#10. ICE Head Wants Deportations to Run Like Amazon Prime: 'We Need to Treat This Like a Business'|Latin Times, 2025]] · [[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#11. The Library of Missing Datasets|Onuoha (2016)]]: Todd Lyons at the Border Security Expo in Phoenix). People handled as parcels: the everyday, bureaucratic form of [[Dehumanization]].

[^ice]: United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement, the federal agency that enforces immigration law. Its acting director from 2025 was Todd Lyons. [Wikipedia: 'U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement)

**The Friction:** When the system is wrong, there is no one to hold to account. In the Dutch ==childcare benefits scandal==[^toeslagen], ==the Tax and Customs Administration's risk model made people without Dutch nationality more likely to be wrongly labelled as fraudsters== ([[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#3. Amnesty ziet mensenrechtenschending bij toeslagenaffaire en vreest voor herhaling|NOS, 2021]]: Amnesty International called it a violation of human rights). A government had handed a decision to a calculation, [[Cybernetic Governance]], and the people it hit could not see why: a [[Black Box]], whose reasoning cannot be examined or challenged. Students met the same: ==from 2012 to 2023 DUO, the agency that pays student finance, selected students with a migration background for fraud checks sooner than others== ([[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#4. Geld terug voor studenten na discriminerende fraudeopsporing|NOS, 2024]]: revealed by NOS op 3 and Investico, and an investigation found indirect discrimination, "unintended and unconscious"). The harm repeats because the system repeats: ==the geographer Sara Safransky calls algorithmic violence repetitive and standardised, and shows how an algorithm that tells a city where to invest turns political choices into technical ones== ([[Safransky-AlgorithmicViolence-2020|Safransky, 2020]]: a market value assessment used by cities across the US, and a new kind of redlining).

[^onuoha]: A Nigerian American artist and academic in New York whose work examines what data collection and technology do to society. [Wikipedia: 'Mimi Onuoha'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimi_Onuoha)
[^toeslagen]: The Tax and Customs Administration used dual nationality in its fraud risk assessments of childcare benefit applicants. In 2021 the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens fined it €2.75 million and called the processing unlawful. [[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#5. Boete voor Belastingdienst van 2,7 miljoen voor discriminatie toeslagenouders|NOS: 'Boete voor Belastingdienst van 2,7 miljoen voor discriminatie toeslagenouders']]

**Why This Matters:** Once you see **algorithmic violence**, a refused benefit or a fraud letter stops looking like bad luck and starts looking like a design. You meet it in the letter that says the system has selected you. What you can ask then is who made the rule, and how to reach a person who can change it. It can be undone: ==the government repaid the fines and reclaimed grants of around ten thousand students, €61 million in all== ([[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#4. Geld terug voor studenten na discriminerende fraudeopsporing|NOS, 2024]]: November 2024). And since 2024 the EU's AI Act [classes systems that decide on public benefits as high-risk](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/ "Read Annex III of the AI Act, point 5(a)."), rules the [[Brussels Effect]] may carry beyond Europe ([[Secondary-AlgorithmicViolence#8. The Brussels Effect and Artificial Intelligence|Siegmann & Anderljung, 2022]]: especially through large US technology companies).
