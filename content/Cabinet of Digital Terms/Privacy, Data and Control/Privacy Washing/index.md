***The app says your privacy matters. How would you know if it didn't?***

**Literal meaning:** *Privacy* is the right to keep part of your life to yourself. *Washing* comes from *whitewashing*, painting over a stain so that the wall looks clean while the stain stays underneath. *Greenwashing* carried the word over to environmental claims, and **[[Privacy Washing|privacy washing]]** carries it over to personal data. Where greenwashing paints its logos with green and nature to look healthy and sustainable, [[Privacy Washing|privacy washing]] uses the lock as its icon and a promise of safety to make people feel secure, a marketing trick built on signs that are easy to show and hard to check. It names a company that talks about protecting your privacy instead of protecting it. Its statements, settings, badges and adverts promise more than the practice behind them.

**Origin:** The term borrows the logic of [[Greenwashing|greenwashing]], environmental claims that go further than environmental practice. It gained ground as corporate privacy promises were followed, one after another, by documented breaches. Facebook gave assurances about user data before ==the Cambridge Analytica revelations in 2018== ([[Secondary-PrivacyWashing#5. Revealed: 50 million Facebook profiles harvested for Cambridge Analytica in major data breach|Cadwalladr & Graham-Harrison, 2018]]: The Guardian, about 50 million profiles). Google offered settings that kept recording where people went after they had switched them off. ==The word itself turned up in law documents in California and Canada before researchers took it up== ([[Cirucci-PrivacyWashing-2024|Cirucci, 2024]]: a study of phone adverts by Apple and Google and their privacy policies).

> Talking about privacy instead of protecting it, with reassurance in place of the practice it describes.

**The Appeal:** Privacy sells. People prefer products that seem to protect them, regulators want to see compliance, and a reassuring sentence costs far less than changing how data is collected. The statements are rarely false in a narrow sense. Their promises are often true, just not in the way you hear them, as when Mark Zuckerberg told the US Senate in 2018 that Facebook ==does not sell data to advertisers==[^zuck].

[^zuck]: True in the narrow sense: Facebook keeps the data and sells advertisers the chance to reach the people it describes, for example women who like skiing. What people heard was that their data was safe. The sentence has its own entry: [["We have never and will never sell user data."]]. [[Secondary-PrivacyWashing#10. Mark Zuckerberg: We do not sell data to advertisers|TechCrunch: 'Mark Zuckerberg: We do not sell data to advertisers']]

**The Friction:** You wouldn't. You are told your data is safe, and you have no way to check. ==In January 2019 Apple put up a billboard in Las Vegas that read What happens on your iPhone, stays on your iPhone== ([[Secondary-PrivacyWashing#1. Apple burns Google in giant billboard touting privacy at CES|Seattle Times, 2019]]: next to the CES technology fair, aimed at Google). ==That summer contractors working for Apple turned out to be listening to fragments of Siri recordings, including conversations recorded by accident== ([[Secondary-PrivacyWashing#2. Apple contractors 'regularly hear confidential details' on Siri recordings|Hern, 2019]]: The Guardian, based on one of the contractors). The adverts of both Apple and Google show the phone as a safe: in Apple's advert *Oversharing*, ==people say their location, search history and card details out loud in public, and at the end the Apple logo turns into a lock== ([[Cirucci-PrivacyWashing-2024|Cirucci, 2024]]: an analysis of adverts by Apple and Google next to the companies' own privacy policies). The danger in these adverts is a stranger who overhears. What the phone sends to the company and its partners, and what they work out from it, stays out of the picture, and that is the business it protects, [[Surveillance Capitalism]], which sells predictions made from what people do. In the interface [[Dark Patterns]] do the same work, with a consent banner that looks like a choice and is designed for yes, while [[Fair Patterns]] show that the same screen can put the settings in plain view.

**Why This Matters:** Knowing the term **[[Privacy Washing|privacy washing]]** turns a privacy promise into a claim you can test, not a guarantee, and researchers have tested it. ==When they checked 5,102 iPhone apps against what the apps actually sent, two in three had a privacy label that did not match== ([[Xiao-PrivacyWashing-2023|Xiao et al., 2023]]: Apple's App Store labels compared with the apps' code and network traffic). ==On Google Play, the labels of TikTok and Twitter said they shared no personal data, while their own privacy policies said they did== ([[Secondary-PrivacyWashing#8. Mozilla Study: Data Privacy Labels for Most Top Apps in Google Play Store are False or Misleading|Mozilla, 2023]]: the 40 most downloaded apps, label against policy). ==After the reporting Apple apologised, stopped keeping Siri recordings by default and let only users who opt in be listened to== ([[Secondary-PrivacyWashing#4. Apple apologizes for listening to Siri conversations|CNBC, 2019]]: announcement of 28 August 2019). The next time you read *we never sell your data* or *your privacy matters to us*, ask what the sentence rules out, and who still gets the data without it being sold: [[Data Brokers]] trade profiles that companies say they do not sell.
