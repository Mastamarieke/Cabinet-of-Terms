***Your behaviour is the raw material. Your prediction is the product.***

**Literal meaning:** **Surveillance capitalism** is **Shoshana Zuboff**'s term for the economic logic in which human behavioural data, generated by digital activities, devices and platforms, is extracted, processed, and sold as prediction products to advertising and other markets. The raw material is human experience; the product is influence over future behaviour. Advertisers and political campaigns buy those predictions to reach one person at a predicted weak spot: [[Microtargeting]]. Its key term is **behavioural surplus**: the part of the collected data that exceeds what a service needs to function, kept not to improve the product but because it has predictive value of its own. WhatsApp is an example: its messages are end-to-end encrypted, but the app still estimates where a user is from IP addresses and phone number area codes ([TechRadar, 2021](https://www.techradar.com/news/whatsapp-clarifies-policy-updates-is-it-too-late-already)). Jaron Lanier's acronym [[BUMMER]], *Behaviours of Users Modified and Made into an Empire for Rent*, names the same business model from inside the industry.

**Origin:** **Zuboff** had used an older image of surveillance before: in *In the Age of the Smart Machine* (1988) she described the computer-monitored workplace as an information [[Panopticon]], where workers cannot tell when they are being watched. In her first academic paper on **surveillance capitalism**, [[Zuboff-SurveillanceCapitalism-2015|Zuboff (2015)]] read Google's practices through two articles by its chief economist, Hal Varian, and named the power they produce *Big Other*: a name that echoes Big Brother in [[1984]], for a power spread across a global network of computers instead of held by one watcher. The full argument followed in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019). She identifies Google's 2000–2001 decision to use behavioural data from search queries to target advertising, first as a side operation and then as the core business model, as the founding moment. The logic then spread to Facebook, the broader platform economy, and beyond, to insurance, finance, healthcare, and physical infrastructure. It also moved into the street: [["500 million people installed that app in 60 days."|Niantic's Pokémon GO (2016)]] turned millions of players into collectors of location data while they thought they were playing a game, and in 2024 Niantic said that scans made by players were training a "Large Geospatial Model" of the physical world ([Maiberg, 2024](https://www.404media.co/pokemon-go-players-have-unwittingly-trained-ai-to-navigate-the-world/)).

> The economic logic in which human behavioural experience is claimed as raw material, processed into prediction products, and sold; named and analysed by **Shoshana Zuboff** in 2019.

**The Appeal:** **Surveillance capitalism** funds services that people value and use daily. The trade, free services for behavioural data, produces real value for users. It is the same bargain as the [[Attention Economy]], paid in data instead of attention.

**The Friction:** **Zuboff**'s argument is that the trade is not disclosed, not negotiated, and not consensual in any meaningful sense. The system is built to keep the surplus invisible: "Our ignorance is their bliss," as she put it in a 2024 VPRO Tegenlicht interview. People who ask what happens to their data get reassurance instead of protection, which is [[Privacy Washing]]: Google kept recording users' movements after they had switched off Location History, according to a 2018 Associated Press report, and in 2022 settled with 40 US states for $391.5 million for misleading them ([Korn, 2022](https://www.cnn.com/2022/11/14/tech/google-location-tracking-settlement)). Mark Zuckerberg's line [["We have never and will never sell user data."|"we have never and will never sell user data"]] is true in the narrow sense: Meta does not sell the data, it sells access to the people the data describes.

The data does not stay where it was given. A study of 959,000 apps in the US and UK Google Play stores found third-party trackers in most of them, a few companies behind most of the tracking, and apps for children among the worst ([[Binns-SurveillanceCapitalism-2018|Binns et al., 2018]]). Many of those trackers sit outside the EU, which is why European rules try to reach companies wherever they are based: the [[Brussels Effect]]. Nothing had to be hacked for Cambridge Analytica: about 300,000 people installed a personality quiz on Facebook that also gathered data from their friends, and the data of up to 87 million people reached the firm, against Facebook's rules ([Time, 2018](https://time.com/5205314/facebook-cambridge-analytica-breach/)). In January 2025 a hack of the location data company Gravy Analytics showed locations drawn from thousands of ordinary apps, Candy Crush, Tinder and MyFitnessPal among them, often without the app makers knowing; it is one of the largest [[Data Brokers]] and sells to commercial and government clients ([Cox, 2025](https://www.404media.co/candy-crush-tinder-myfitnesspal-see-the-thousands-of-apps-hijacked-to-spy-on-your-location/)). Government is a buyer, and it can become the next owner. In February 2025 Elon Musk's DOGE team, with engineers as young as nineteen, gained access to sensitive government databases and the Treasury's payment system ([Ingram, 2025](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/elon-musk-doge-usaid-treasury-government-rcna190450)), the merger of private infrastructure and public power that [[Muskism]] names. Two months later the acting director of ICE, Todd Lyons, said he wanted deportation to run [["Like Prime, but with human beings."|"like Prime, but with human beings"]] ([MacDonald-Evoy, 2025](https://azmirror.com/2025/04/08/ice-director-envisions-amazon-like-mass-deportation-system-prime-but-with-human-beings/)).

Leaving is harder than staying. Once photos, contacts and habits sit with one company, switching costs more than it is worth, which is [[Vendor Lock-in]], and a company that knows you will not leave can make the service worse: [[Enshittification]], Cory Doctorow's word for it. In 2024 Amazon put advertisements into Prime Video for people who already paid, and charged $2.99 a month to remove them ([Rizzo, 2023](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/09/22/amazon-prime-video-ads-start-in-2024.html)). Who pays is uneven: predictions built on data from an unequal past repeat that inequality at scale, as [[Algorithmic Violence]], harm done by automated systems that no one decided on. What makes it look normal is [[Dataism]], the belief that data is a neutral good, so that taking it is a neutral act. [[Secondary-SurveillanceCapitalism|Van Iperen (2026)]] describes the result as environment design (*omgevingsontwerp*): people are not ordered, they are steered by the space that is built around them. The Chinese [[Social Credit System]] is often held up as the opposite, a state that orders: in 2018 its blacklists stopped people from buying plane tickets 17.5 million times ([Jee, 2019](https://www.technologyreview.com/2019/03/04/136791/chinas-social-credit-system-stopped-millions-of-people-buying-travel-tickets/)). In the West insurers, employers and platforms score behaviour too, without a central list.

Meta's camera glasses, promoted by the [[Influencer]] Kylie Jenner, record the people around the wearer (Hoogenraad, 2026), and two Swedish newspapers reported that wearers' private video goes to workers in Kenya who train the glasses' AI: [[Ghost Work]], done on footage of people who never agreed to be filmed. When Roel Maalderink and Bits of Freedom mocked the glasses in a video, Meta took it off Instagram: [[Deplatforming]], by the maker of the product being criticised. The full story is in [[Biometric Surveillance]].

**Why This Matters:** **Surveillance capitalism** names the economic logic that underlies most of the Platform Mechanisms cluster. Once you see human experience as raw material, the specific mechanisms ([[Recommender Systems|recommender systems]], [[Dark Patterns|dark patterns]], dopamine loops) become legible as components of a single extractive system. The same logic also produces political power: [[Technofeudalism]], Yanis Varoufakis's term, describes platform owners who collect rent from users and businesses with no other option, and [[Broligarchy]] the network of tech owners who turned that commercial success into political influence. **Zuboff** compares the moment to the Gilded Age, when concentrated economic power was eventually reined in by law; the comparison gives hope, and it also sits uneasily with her claim that this power is unprecedented. The question then is not only how one person can opt out, but which rules would make opting out possible at all.
