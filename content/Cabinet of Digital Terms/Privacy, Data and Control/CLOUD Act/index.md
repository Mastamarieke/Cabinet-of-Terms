***Your data is stored in Europe. Does European law protect it?***

**Literal meaning:** **[[CLOUD Act]]** is short for *Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data Act*. An *act* is a law passed by a parliament, here the US Congress, and the long name says what this one does. It makes clear (*clarifying*) when American authorities may legally (*lawful*) use data that is stored abroad (*overseas*). The short name was chosen so that its letters spell *cloud*, the everyday word for data kept on someone else's servers, which sounds weightless and everywhere. In practice the cloud is a server in a building, owned by a company in a particular country, and the law makes clear that this country decides. Since 2018 it has obliged American technology companies to hand over data on a US warrant, wherever in the world the server stands.

**Origin:** The law answered the *Microsoft Ireland* case, in which US prosecutors demanded emails that Microsoft kept on servers in Dublin, and Microsoft refused, arguing that a US warrant stops at the border. ==Political geographers describe the case as the point where the place in which data are stored collided with the question of whose law reaches them== ([[Glasze-CLOUDAct-2022|Glasze et al., 2022]]: a forum on digital sovereignty in the journal Geopolitics). Congress passed the **[[CLOUD Act]]** in March 2018 while the case was before the Supreme Court, and the case was dropped.

> A US law that makes American cloud companies hand over data to US authorities, wherever the data is stored.

**The Appeal:** For investigators the law solves a real problem. Evidence of crimes is often stored on servers abroad, and until 2018 a warrant stopped where the server did. Europe wants the same reach: a month after the [[CLOUD Act]], ==the European Commission proposed its own rules to obtain electronic evidence directly from service providers, and since 2019 the EU and the US have negotiated an agreement on access to each other's data== ([[Christakis-CLOUDAct-2021|Christakis & Terpan, 2021]]: International Data Privacy Law, on the EU–US negotiations and where they clash with EU law).

**The Friction:** Not on its own: a server in Europe does not keep your data under European law alone, because what counts is where the company that owns it is based. In June 2025 the director of legal affairs of Microsoft France was asked under oath in the French Senate whether he could guarantee that French data would never reach US authorities without France's consent. ==He said he could not, adding that it had never happened yet== ([[Secondary-CLOUDAct#2. Compte rendu de la commission d'enquête sur la commande publique, 10 juin 2025|Sénat, 2025]]: hearing of 10 June 2025 on public procurement). That collides with the GDPR, the European privacy law that the [[Brussels Effect]] carries far beyond Europe, so a European school, hospital or ministry that works with Microsoft, Amazon or Google keeps its data in Europe and still falls under a US warrant. Leaving is hard when the email, files and accounts of a whole organisation run on one supplier, a form of [[Vendor Lock-in]]. How far that dependence reaches became visible in The Hague, where ==after US sanctions in February 2025 the chief prosecutor of the International Criminal Court lost his Microsoft email address, according to court staff== ([[Secondary-CLOUDAct#3. Trump's sanctions on ICC's chief prosecutor have halted tribunal's work, officials and lawyers say|AP, 2025]]: Microsoft did not respond). That was a sanction rather than a warrant, but it ran the same way, through an American supplier into a European institution, the dependence that [[Sovereignty as a Service]] describes: renting the systems you work with from a company you cannot fire.

**Why This Matters:** The **[[CLOUD Act]]** shows that *stored in Europe* is only half an answer, for the school or work account you were given as much as for a ministry. ==France only certifies a cloud service as trusted if the company behind it is subject to EU law alone, with its headquarters in the EU and non-European shareholders in the minority== ([[Secondary-CLOUDAct#4. FAQ avant de se lancer dans la qualification SecNumCloud|ANSSI, n.d.]]: the French cybersecurity agency on its SecNumCloud qualification). The next time you are told your data stays in Europe, ask who owns the system and what happens if a US warrant arrives; if the owner is an American company, European storage does not settle it.
