***The child talks about the YouTuber as if they know them. They feel they do. The YouTuber does not know the child exists.***

**Literal meaning:** A **parasocial relationship** is a one-sided emotional bond in which one person — the audience member — feels familiarity, affection, and connection toward a media figure who has no reciprocal awareness of them. The term was coined by sociologists Donald Horton and Richard Wohl in 1956 to describe the experience of television audiences toward presenters.

**Origin:** Horton and Wohl published their foundational paper "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction" in *Psychiatry* in 1956, identifying the phenomenon of viewers feeling personal intimacy with television personalities. The concept was extensively developed in media psychology across subsequent decades. In the digital age, parasocial relationships have intensified: **YouTube**, Twitch, and **TikTok** creators speak directly to camera, share personal details, and respond to comments — creating a richer simulation of personal relationship than television permitted.

> A one-sided emotional bond in which the audience member experiences genuine affection for a figure who does not know they exist.

**The Appeal:** Parasocial relationships are psychologically real and not inherently harmful. They provide models for social behaviour, a sense of connection and belonging, and entertainment with an emotional register that pure fiction does not provide. For children and adolescents, parasocial bonds with YouTubers and gamers are a normal part of media engagement.

**The Friction:** The intensity differential is the structural problem. [[Recommender Systems]] amplify parasocial bonds by surfacing the same creator repeatedly, building familiarity through repetition. [[Family Vlogging]] and [[Kidfluencer]] content create parasocial relationships between adult audiences and children — which is the [[Online Grooming]] risk vector: someone who feels they know a child from their online presence, and who uses that apparent intimacy to initiate contact. The **creator economy** also exploits parasocial bonds commercially: merchandise, membership platforms, and donation systems all depend on audiences who feel personal investment in a creator they have never met. [[Virtual Influencer]] — a computer-generated persona engineered to behave like a human creator — is the logical extension: the parasocial bond is activated by design, without any person behind it. The bond is real; the subject of it cannot exist outside the studio. The inverse mechanism is equally engineered: low production values and apparent rawness can activate the same parasocial bond through the impression of authenticity. Van de Griend & Pottjewijd (2026) document this in "decline porn" vlogging — shaky footage, unscripted confrontations, the aesthetic of ongefilterde werkelijkheid — where the roughness is the trust signal, building parasocial intimacy while carrying audiences into far-right content.

**Why This Matters:** **Parasocial relationship** names the emotional mechanism that makes digital media intimately engaging. [[Mimetic Desire]] explains why it works: the creator functions as a model — showing followers what is worth wanting, how to live, what to feel — and the follower imitates, without any reciprocal relationship existing. Once you know the term, "they feel like a friend" and "I feel like they know me" are both descriptions of a designed relationship structure.
