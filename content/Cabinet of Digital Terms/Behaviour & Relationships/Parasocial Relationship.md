***The child talks about the YouTuber as if they know them. They feel they do. The YouTuber does not know the child exists.***

**Literal meaning:** A **parasocial relationship** is a one-sided emotional bond in which one person — the audience member — feels familiarity, affection, and connection toward a media figure who has no reciprocal awareness of them. The term was coined by sociologists Donald Horton and Richard Wohl in 1956 to describe the experience of television audiences toward presenters.

**Origin:** Horton and Wohl published their foundational paper "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction" in *Psychiatry* in 1956, identifying the phenomenon of viewers feeling personal intimacy with television personalities. The concept was extensively developed in media psychology across subsequent decades. In the digital age, parasocial relationships have intensified: **YouTube**, Twitch, and **TikTok** creators speak directly to camera, share personal details, and respond to comments — creating a richer simulation of personal relationship than television permitted.

> A one-sided emotional bond in which the audience member experiences genuine affection for a figure who does not know they exist.

**The Appeal:** Parasocial relationships are psychologically real and not inherently harmful. They provide models for social behaviour, a sense of connection and belonging, and entertainment with an emotional register that pure fiction does not provide. For children and adolescents, parasocial bonds with YouTubers and gamers are a normal part of media engagement.

**The Friction:** The intensity differential is the structural problem. [[Recommender Systems]] amplify parasocial bonds by surfacing the same creator repeatedly, building familiarity through repetition. [[Dopamine Feedback Loops]] supply the rhythm of that repetition: the bond deepens by being renewed on a schedule the platform sets, not by anything the creator does for the individual follower. [[Family Vlogging]] and [[Kidfluencer]] content create parasocial relationships between adult audiences and children — which is the [[Online Grooming]] risk vector: someone who feels they know a child from their online presence, and who uses that apparent intimacy to initiate contact. The [[Creator Economy]] also exploits parasocial bonds commercially: merchandise, membership platforms, and donation systems all depend on audiences who feel personal investment in a creator they have never met. [[Virtual Influencer]] — a computer-generated persona engineered to behave like a human creator — is the logical extension: the parasocial bond is activated by design, without any person behind it. The bond is real; the subject of it cannot exist outside the studio. The inverse mechanism is equally engineered: low production values and apparent rawness can activate the same parasocial bond through the impression of authenticity. Van de Griend & Pottjewijd (2026) document this in "decline porn" vlogging — shaky footage, unscripted confrontations, the aesthetic of ongefilterde werkelijkheid — where the roughness is the trust signal, building parasocial intimacy while carrying audiences into far-right content.

Where the bond is engineered, it can also be sold outright. Chinese users have a word for the market built on that demand: the **loneliness economy**, an industry of romance games, AI companion apps, and actors hired by the day to play a customer's favourite fictional character in person. Blussé (2026) reports that the Chinese market for romance games reached 8 billion yuan (around one billion euro) in 2024, more than twice the year before, and that the companion app Xingye, known outside China as Talkie, has over twenty million active users who spend more than an hour a day in it. There is a word for the customer as well: *mengnü*, dream girl, used in China for a woman who imagines a relationship with a fictional character, a celebrity or an AI lover. What she names as the attraction is the asymmetry itself: a fictional boyfriend cannot cheat, lie, take her money or turn violent. Horton and Wohl described one-sidedness as the limitation of the form. Here it is the product.

The bond is gendered on both sides. Practically every actor hired to play a boyfriend is a woman, because that is what clients consider safe: at a date with a woman, one of them explains, you have nothing to fear, and the affection is purer. That also breaks the structure Horton and Wohl described, because this media figure does know the customer exists. The actor is present, responsive and paid, and she can be changed by the encounter herself: the one quoted here remembers not daring to hold her client's hand on her first booking, until the client took hers.

Beijing now treats the bond as a regulated product. Rules for "anthropomorphic interactions" in AI products, in force since 15 July 2026, forbid apps from encouraging "emotional dependency", ban them outright for minors, cap stored conversation history at three months, and require a reminder every two hours that the partner is not real. A regulator that limits memory and prescribes interruption is treating [[AI Dependency]] as a matter of dosage, and the parasocial bond as real enough to legislate. Users read a second motive into it: with marriage and birth rates falling, a woman who prefers a fictional partner becomes a matter of state interest. That is the [[Pro-natalism]] anxiety arriving through a Chinese regulator rather than a Silicon Valley manifesto.

[[AI Literacy]] is noticing for yourself that the "warmth" of a companion app is engineered rather than felt. Beijing does not rely on that: it obliges the app to say so every two hours. The reminder does the work the insight would have done. [[Digital Detox]] works on the supply instead, interrupting the schedule that keeps the bond renewed.

**Why This Matters:** **Parasocial relationship** names the emotional mechanism that makes digital media intimately engaging. [[Mimetic Desire]] explains why it works: the creator functions as a model — showing followers what is worth wanting, how to live, what to feel — and the follower imitates, without any reciprocal relationship existing. Once you know the term, "they feel like a friend" and "I feel like they know me" are both descriptions of a designed relationship structure.
