## Gaucher, Friesen & Kay (2011) — Evidence That Gendered Wording in Job Advertisements Exists and Sustains Gender Inequality

**Gaucher, D., Friesen, J. & Kay, A.C. (2011).** Evidence That Gendered Wording in Job Advertisements Exists and Sustains Gender Inequality. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 101(1), 109–128. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022530

---

### What this source contributes

Five studies, two archival and three experimental. Studies 1 and 2 sampled real job advertisements and found that postings for male-dominated occupations used more masculine-coded words (*leader*, *competitive*, *dominant*) than postings for female-dominated ones, while feminine-coded words (*support*, *understand*, *interpersonal*) appeared at the same rate in both. Studies 3 to 5 constructed advertisements with more masculine than feminine wording: readers pictured more men in those jobs, and women found the jobs less appealing. Study 5 located the mechanism. The effect ran through belongingness — women reading masculine wording felt they would not fit — and not through perceived skill; they did not think themselves less able, they thought themselves less welcome.

The authors frame this within social dominance theory as an institutional-level mechanism that maintains inequality without anyone intending it: the words do the sorting before an application is written.

### Analytical function in AI-Ninja

The paper never mentions *ninja*. What it supplies is the mechanism by which a word like it works. The link to the word itself comes from the secondary bundle: Textio's analysis of fifty million job postings, reported by *Fortune* in 2016, placed *ninja* and *rockstar* among the terms after which women stop applying. Together they turn the entry's Origin from an observation about romantic vocabulary into a claim about who that vocabulary recruits and who it turns away, and they give the entry its second axis alongside the obsolescence of the skill.
