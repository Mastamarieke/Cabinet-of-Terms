***He was removed from the platform. His follower count on the remaining platforms increased.***

**Literal meaning:** **Deplatforming** describes the removal of a person, account, or community from a digital platform, through a ban, a suspension or the closing of an account, usually because the platform says its rules on hate speech or its terms of service were broken. High-profile deplatformings include the removal of Alex Jones from multiple platforms in 2018 and Donald Trump from Twitter/X in January 2021.

**Origin:** The term has been in use in activist contexts since at least the 1990s, when "no-platforming" meant refusing controversial figures a place to speak. It gained its current digital meaning with the scaling of social media and the development of platform content policies. The Alex Jones **deplatforming** in 2018 was the first coordinated multi-platform removal and generated the most substantial body of research on its effects.

> The removal of a person from a digital platform to moderate content: it reduces their reach, and whether it works in the long run is disputed.

**The Appeal:** **Deplatforming** removes harmful content and actors from high-visibility spaces. Research on the Alex Jones **deplatforming** found significant reductions in his cross-platform audience and search traffic in the immediate aftermath. For platforms, removal protects advertisers, other users, and brand reputation. For researchers of extremism, it disrupts network effects.

**The Friction:** The evidence on long-term effectiveness is genuinely mixed. Research by Robert Faris and colleagues at Harvard found that deplatformed users often migrate to smaller, less moderated platforms where they reach more radicalised audiences. The [[Manosphere]], the network of anti-feminist forums and channels, shows the limit. Meta, TikTok and YouTube removed Andrew Tate in 2022, yet in 2024 researchers at Dublin City University found him among the most frequent figures in the videos shown to new accounts of teenage boys. Removing an account, Debbie Ging said, does not remove the content ([O'Brien, 2024](https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/education/2024/04/17/teenage-boys-bombarded-with-misogynist-content-within-minutes-of-signing-up-to-tiktok-and-youtube-shorts/)). [[Flood the Zone]], filling the information space so that nothing can be checked, sometimes gains from **deplatforming**: the removal itself becomes news and spreads the removed person's message. [[Deep State]], the belief that institutions are secretly hostile, grows when a removal looks like coordinated suppression. Some see the same removal as necessary protection and others as censorship. Twitter suspended Donald Trump permanently on 8 January 2021, "due to the risk of further incitement of violence" ([Allyn, 2021](https://www.npr.org/2021/01/08/954760928/twitter-bans-president-trump-citing-risk-of-further-incitement-of-violence)). In November 2022 the new owner, Elon Musk, put the account back after a poll on Twitter in which 51.8 percent voted for it ([Duffy, 2022](https://www.cnn.com/2022/11/19/business/twitter-musk-trump-reinstate)). The decision lay with one man; Quinn Slobodian and Ben Tarnoff call that kind of private control over public infrastructure [[Muskism]]. A ban at least can be seen and argued about. [[Shadow Banning]], reducing an account's reach without telling its owner, cannot: a user cannot object to a decision they do not know about. Under the European Digital Services Act a platform must now explain why it removes, suspends or demotes content, and must offer a free way to object. The rules apply to every platform with users in the EU, wherever it is based: the [[Brussels Effect]] ([Articles 17 and 20](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).

**Why This Matters:** **Deplatforming** makes visible the limits of content moderation as a response to political violence. Removing an account reduces its reach, but the people and their ideas often move to other platforms.
