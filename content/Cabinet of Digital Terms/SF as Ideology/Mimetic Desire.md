***You think you want it. But watch who taught you to want it.***

**Literal meaning:** **Mimetic desire** is the theory that human desire is not autonomous but imitative: we do not choose what to want independently, but learn to want by observing others — a model, a rival, an influencer. The word derives from the Greek *mimesis* (imitation). The theory proposes that virtually all desire is triangular: subject, model, and object — rather than the simpler picture of a subject simply wanting a thing. Remove the model, and the desire collapses.

**Origin:** The concept was developed by French literary critic and anthropologist René Girard, first in *Deceit, Desire, and the Novel* (1961), where he argued that the great European novelists — Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Proust, Dostoevsky — had all understood, without naming it, that their characters desired through models. Girard extended the theory into anthropology in *Violence and the Sacred* (1972): when mimetic desire spreads through a group and two or more rivals compete for the same object, **mimetic rivalry** escalates. As rivalry spreads and threatens social cohesion, the group resolves the crisis by uniting against a single victim — the **scapegoat**. The victim is chosen somewhat arbitrarily; what matters is the unifying function of collective exclusion. The pleasure of participating in that exclusion — *het heerlijke gevoel dat je aan de goede kant staat* — is the emotional signature of the scapegoat mechanism in action.

> We don't desire on our own — we imitate the desires of others. Shared models create rivals. Outsiders become scapegoats.

**The Appeal:** The framework explains both the smallest and the largest dynamics with the same mechanism. Why does a child want the toy another child holds? Mimetic desire. Why do financial bubbles form and collapse? Mimetic desire. Why do online mobs converge with sudden unanimity on a single target? The scapegoat mechanism. Peter Thiel — who studied under Girard at Stanford and founded the Imitatio Foundation to promote his work — drew a direct business conclusion: if desire is mimetic, competition is a pathology. Two companies fighting over the same market are engaged in mimetic rivalry. The rational move is to avoid imitation entirely: differentiate completely, seek monopoly, go *zero to one*. Girard became the philosophical foundation for Thiel's entire worldview — and, through Thiel, for a significant strand of Silicon Valley strategy.

**The Friction:** The framework risks explaining too much. When every desire can be attributed to mimetic imitation, the theory becomes difficult to falsify — a lens that fits all data. Critics note that Girard's scapegoat mechanism, inflected by Christian theology, carries assumptions about sacrificial violence that are not universally shared. And Thiel's application extracts the individual survival logic while discarding Girard's deeper point: the scapegoat mechanism is a pattern to be recognised and resisted, not a system to be gamed. Founding a surveillance company (Palantir), an autonomous weapons firm (Anduril), and a Tolkien-branded investment network while claiming to act against mimetic conformity is a contradiction Girard would have recognised immediately.

**Why This Matters:** There is a crucial difference between a celebrity you admire from a distance and a peer you compete with directly. Historically, those two categories were separate: a film star was too far away to be a rival. Social media collapses that distance. An influencer feels like a peer — approachable, responsive, relatable — while simultaneously functioning as a model showing you what is worth wanting. The result: everyone becomes a potential rival. Every like is a status signal. Every unboxing video is a desire prompt.

**Mimetic desire** names the engine beneath much of what digital platforms are built on. [[Influencer]] culture is mimetic desire as infrastructure: you want what they have because they are your model. [[Recommender Systems]] surface what others watched, bought, endorsed — constantly refreshing your model. [[Attention Economy]] is sustained by mimetic anxiety: the fear of desiring the wrong thing, being behind, missing out. [[FOMO]] is mimetic desire made anxious. And the scapegoat mechanism explains online pile-ons with uncomfortable precision: the sudden unanimity, the disproportionate intensity, the relief of being on the right side.
