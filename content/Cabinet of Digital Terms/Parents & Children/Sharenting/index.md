***The photo was posted before the child could speak. By the time they are old enough to object, there are thousands.***

**Literal meaning:** **Sharenting**, from *sharing* and *parenting*, is parents posting photos, videos and details of their children's lives online, from the occasional family picture to a childhood documented as content. *Sharing* is the platforms' own verb and sounds like generosity; *parenting* is care and protection. Joined, they describe care carried out as publication, and the word quietly hides who is being shared: not the parent, but the child.

**Origin:** The word is usually traced to the *Wall Street Journal*, where in 2012 Steven Leckart described other parents' "oversharenting". The legal scholar [[Steinberg-Sharenting-2017|Stacey Steinberg (2017)]] gave it its academic grounding, arguing that children have a right to decide what is known about them.

> The construction of a child's digital identity by a parent before the child can consent — producing a permanent public record the child did not choose.

**The Appeal:** Sharing a child's moments is an ordinary expression of love and a way to stay close to family far away. The impulse is genuine, and the likes and congratulations are real. For many parents, **sharenting** is simply what parenting looks like now.

**The Friction:** The child cannot consent, and the record stays. By the age of thirteen, the [[Secondary-Sharenting|Children's Commissioner for England (2018)]] estimated, a child has had 1,300 photos and videos posted by their parents. Parents are not careless about this: [[BlumRoss-Sharenting-2017|Blum-Ross and Livingstone (2017)]], interviewing seventeen parent bloggers, found them weighing what is their "story to tell", and responsible for guarding their children against risks their own posts create. [[Personal Branding]] is something adults do to themselves; here it is done to someone who cannot yet speak, and [[Momfluencer]] and [[Family Vlogging]] turn it into income. The photos also leave the family. A public face is raw material for [[Biometric Surveillance]], and since chatbots such as Grok began altering ordinary photos of women and minors on request, [[Synthetic Generation]] means a holiday picture can be turned into something else by anyone. [[Screen Time]] shows the same parent facing the other way: parental controls watch the child, sharenting shows the child to others, and in neither case does the child have a say.

Older children have begun to object. In 2017 a sixteen-year-old in Rome ([[Secondary-Sharenting|Materi, 2018]]) took his mother to court over the photos she had posted during his parents' divorce, and won. Dutch courts have ruled for younger children, and in 2024 France gave family judges the power to stop a parent publishing a child's images.

**Why This Matters:** **Sharenting** makes visible who owns a child's digital identity before they are old enough to have an opinion about it. Once you see the data infrastructure the images enter, the decision to post looks different.
