***The photo was posted before the child could speak. By the time they are old enough to object, there are thousands.***

**Literal meaning:** **Sharenting** — a portmanteau of *sharing* and *parenting* — describes the practice of parents sharing images, videos, and personal information about their children on social media. The term covers a spectrum from occasional family photos to systematic documentation of a child's life as content. The two halves do different work. *Sharing* is the platforms' own verb and sounds like generosity, giving something of yourself; *parenting* is care and protection. Joined, they describe care carried out as publication, and the word quietly changes who is being shared: not the parent, but the child.

**Origin:** The term is usually traced to the *Wall Street Journal*, where in May 2012 the journalist Steven Leckart described the "oversharenting" of other parents in "The Facebook-Free Baby". It spread as smartphone photography made constant documentation and sharing frictionless, and entered the Collins English Dictionary in 2016. The legal scholar Stacey Steinberg gave it its foundational academic treatment in "Sharenting: Children's Privacy in the Age of Social Media" (*Emory Law Journal*, 2017), arguing that children have a right to informational self-determination that **sharenting** may compromise.

> The construction of a child's digital identity by a parent before the child can consent — producing a permanent public record the child did not choose.

**The Appeal:** Sharing children's moments is a normal expression of parental love and a way to maintain connection with family and friends at distance. The impulse is genuine, the technology is frictionless, and the social rewards — likes, congratulations, engagement — are real. For many parents, **sharenting** is simply what parenting in a connected world looks like.

**The Friction:** The child cannot consent. The digital footprint created in childhood is permanent and may follow the child into adulthood: searchable, screenshottable, potentially embarrassing or harmful. [[Surveillance Capitalism]] — human behaviour as raw material — means that images of children feed algorithmic training data, advertising systems, and facial recognition databases. [[Momfluencer]] and [[Family Vlogging]] are the commercial intensification: **sharenting** at scale, as business model. [[Personal Branding]] is something adults do to themselves; here it is done to someone who cannot yet speak, and [[Kidfluencer]] is where that brand starts to earn. [[Highlight Reel]] names the format of what gets posted: family life chosen and arranged before anyone outside the family sees it. [[Screen Time]] shows the same parent facing the other way: parental controls watch the child, sharenting shows the child to others, and in neither case does the child have a say. The Children's Commissioner for England estimated in 2018 that by the age of thirteen a child has had 1,300 photos and videos of themselves posted by their parents. Those images do not stay in the family album: a public photograph of a face is the raw material of [[Biometric Surveillance]], the kind of image facial recognition companies scrape from public profiles. They can also be remade. In January 2026 *The Guardian* reported that users of X were asking the platform's chatbot Grok to turn ordinary photos of real women, and of minors, into sexualised images, and that Grok often obliged within minutes; nearly three-quarters of a sample of some 500 such posts were requests of that kind. [[Synthetic Generation]] means that a holiday photo posted once can be altered by anyone, without asking the person in it.

Every post is rewarded with likes and reactions, and the person in the picture cannot yet refuse. Older children have started to. In December 2017 a court in Rome ruled for a sixteen-year-old who had taken his mother to court for posting his photos alongside her Facebook attacks on his father, during their divorce; she had to take them down, with 10,000 euros at stake. Dutch courts have ruled on behalf of younger children. In May 2020 a court in Gelderland ordered a grandmother to remove photos of her grandchildren posted without their mother's consent, which the GDPR requires for children under sixteen. Two months later the Arnhem-Leeuwarden court of appeal barred a mother from posting photos of her six-year-old daughter on Facebook and Instagram for five years without the father's consent. A child that age, the court reasoned, cannot foresee the consequences, and the platform's own terms give it a worldwide licence to use whatever is posted. In February 2024 France made protecting a child's private life an explicit part of parental authority, and allowed a family judge to restrict a parent's right to publish images of the child.

**Why This Matters:** **Sharenting** makes visible who owns a child's digital identity before they are old enough to have an opinion about it. Once you see the data infrastructure the images enter, the decision to post looks different.
