***"Dark patterns" sounds like a design critique. "Deceptive design" sounds like a legal charge. The EU chose the second one deliberately.***

**Literal meaning:** **Deceptive design** describes interface and product design choices that mislead users — creating false impressions, hiding information, making unwanted actions easy and desired actions difficult, or exploiting cognitive biases to override users' stated preferences. The term is used in academic research and legal contexts in preference to the more colloquial "**dark patterns**."

**Origin:** "**Dark patterns**" was coined by UX designer **Harry Brignull** in 2010. Researchers including **Colin Gray**, Cristiana Santos, and Nataliia Bielova developed the academic vocabulary, with "**deceptive design**" gaining traction as the legally more precise framing. Brignull moved too: his own catalogue, once darkpatterns.org, is now deceptive.design, and he calls the practices **deceptive patterns**. Three names are therefore in use for the same interfaces — *dark patterns* from 2010, *deceptive patterns* from the person who named them, and *deceptive design* from the regulators — and the distance between the first and the last is the distance between a complaint and a charge. The EU's Digital Services Act (2022) and the FTC in the United States began using **deceptive design** as a regulatory category. The European Data Protection Board issued guidelines on **deceptive design** in 2022. The shift from "**dark patterns**" to "**deceptive design**" matters: it moves the frame from aesthetics to law.

> The legally preferred term for interface design that misleads users — chosen over **dark patterns** because it assigns responsibility and enables enforcement.

**The Appeal:** The academic and legal precision of "**deceptive design**" enables regulatory action that "**dark patterns**" — a designer's colloquial term — does not. The current definition also reaches further than the web page it was written for: apps, websites **and AI systems** that stop you doing what you want, which brings [[Sycophancy (AI)]] and [[AI Dependency]] within range of a legal category rather than a design critique. Once **deceptive design** is a legal category, companies can be fined, not just criticised. The EU has begun doing exactly that: GDPR enforcement has produced multi-million euro fines for cookie consent designs that made refusal significantly harder than acceptance.

**The Friction:** The legal category is only as effective as enforcement capacity. [[Dark Patterns]] — the colloquial vocabulary — documents the practices; **deceptive design** frames them as actionable. But [[Vendor Lock-in]] — private companies managing public infrastructure — means that the most egregious **deceptive design** is often deployed in contexts where regulatory reach is weakest. [[Nudging]] — behaviour steering through environmental design — occupies the legally ambiguous zone between **deceptive design** and legitimate persuasion: the line between informing and manipulating is contested, and platforms exploit the ambiguity. [[Fair Patterns]] is the positive formulation: design that achieves the same outcomes through honest means. Behind fair patterns stands a method. [[VSD (Value Sensitive Design)|Value Sensitive Design]], developed by Batya Friedman from 1996, asks before anything is built which values a system embeds, and whose.

**Why This Matters:** **Deceptive design** makes the vocabulary shift analytically significant. The same interface can be described as a dark pattern (a design criticism), a **deceptive design** (a legal category), or a business feature (a commercial decision). Which frame applies determines what consequences follow.
