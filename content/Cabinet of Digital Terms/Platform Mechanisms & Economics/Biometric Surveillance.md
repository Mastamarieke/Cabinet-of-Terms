***The camera does not just see you. It measures you — your face, your pace, your pulse — and stores the result.***

**Literal meaning:** **Biometric surveillance** is the collection, processing, and analysis of physical or behavioural characteristics — facial features, fingerprints, iris patterns, gait, voice, heartrate — to identify, track, or make inferences about individuals. In the platform context, it includes facial recognition in consumer devices, emotion detection in advertising systems, and the use of physiological signals as engagement and targeting data.

**Origin:** Biometric measurement arrived in ordinary life as authentication. Apple's Touch ID (2013) and Face ID (2017) made the body a key and kept the measurement on the device, which made the practice familiar before it became public. What has changed since is the direction it points: the same measurement that confirms a phone's owner can, turned outward, establish who a stranger is. Shoshana Zuboff (2019) placed that turn in an economic frame, with the body as a source of data that exceeds what any service needs to function.

> The collection and analysis of physical body signals — face, gait, heartrate — to identify, track, or make inferences about people.

**The Appeal:** Biometric authentication is genuinely convenient and secure. Face ID unlocks a phone faster and more securely than a PIN. Fitness trackers that monitor heartrate provide useful health data. The consumer applications are real and valued.

**The Friction:** The same technology that unlocks your phone can identify you in a public space without your knowledge. [[Surveillance Capitalism]] — human behaviour as raw material — extends to physical presence when biometric data is collected passively. [[Deadnaming]] is one specific consequence: facial recognition systems may identify a trans person by their former name, which is retained in databases they cannot access or correct. [[Algorithmic Violence]] — harm caused by automated systems without individual decisions — describes what happens when biometric systems make consequential errors: misidentification in criminal justice systems has led to wrongful arrests. In September 2024 the Dutch Data Protection Authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million for a database of more than fifty billion photographs of faces, Dutch faces among them, scraped from the internet without the knowledge of the people in them and sold to intelligence and investigative services. Dutch organisations may not use it. Clearview did not respond to the investigation or contest the fine, and the regulator said it expected the company would not pay. The EU AI Act (2024) restricts real-time **biometric surveillance** in public spaces — a recognition that the technology requires specific governance. Van Iperen (2026) makes the architecture legible through parallel: the Halter livestock system collects 6,000 data points per animal per minute — location, movement, sleep, temperature, social interaction, eating, excretion — and the smartphone does the same for humans. *"Als een soort halsband leest de telefoon gedrag uit en geeft prikkels terug"*: the phone reads behaviour and returns stimuli. The body is the sensor; the feed, notification, or health advice is the correction signal sent back.

Camera glasses are where the turn outward becomes ordinary equipment. Meta reported seven million pairs sold in 2025, and Dutch opticians stock them. Harvard students showed in 2024 that an off-the-shelf pair could be wired to facial recognition; Meta had code for a *Name Tag* feature sitting on the glasses, removed it after it came to light in early 2026, and filed a patent that August for a function that would identify and record people. Agents of the US immigration service ICE have used the glasses to film demonstrators. Under the EU AI Act such a feature would make the glasses a high-risk system requiring certification, but Anne Meuwese (Leiden) points out that the standards Meta would have to meet are not finished, and that more than a year would pass before a fine became possible. And the signal everyone is arguing about covers half the device. The light reports the camera; nothing reports the microphones, which have to be listening for the assistant to answer to a voice at all. Voice sits in the list at the top of this entry alongside face and gait, which makes it the one biometric these glasses can pick up with no indication of any kind — from the wearer's companions as readily as from strangers, and from a conversation rather than a glance. The glasses are not in fact silent, only silent to people: they announce themselves over Bluetooth with a manufacturer signature, and an independent developer used that to build ZuckOff, an app that listens for the broadcast and warns that a pair is nearby. Its own documentation is careful — some models go quiet while worn, and a detection is not proof that anything is being recorded — but the asymmetry it exposes is the point. The device emits a machine-readable identifier continuously while offering people a small light, and the warning system that uses the identifier was built by someone else. Knowing whether you are on camera has become a thing you install, which is [[Privacy as a Premium]] arriving in the street rather than in a settings menu. The same invisibility has a use: [[Ragebaiting]] no longer needs a willing second party, because the person supplying the outrage can be filmed without knowing it.

**Why This Matters:** **Biometric surveillance** makes the body readable. Once your face is a data point, your presence in a space generates a record you did not choose to create. The body is no longer private by default.
