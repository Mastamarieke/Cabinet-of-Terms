## Ardoline & Lenzo (2025) — The cognitive and moral harms of platform decay

**Ardoline, M. J. & Lenzo, E. (2025). The cognitive and moral harms of platform decay.** *Ethics and Information Technology*, 27(3), 37. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-025-09846-1 (online since 26 July 2025)

---

### What this source contributes

Two philosophers argue that the harm of platform decay is larger than economic unfairness or inconvenience. They use *platform decay* as a synonym for Doctorow's *enshittification*. Their argument runs in three steps.

First, platforms such as Google search are part of how people think, in the sense of the extended mind (Clark and Chalmers): like the notebook that Otto, a man with a failing memory, carries everywhere. People increasingly remember where to find information rather than the information itself.

Second, when such a platform decays, for instance when ads take the place of the most relevant search results, it becomes what Timms and Spurrett call *hostile scaffolding*, a support that now works for someone else. Through the Ethical Parity Principle (Levy) they compare this to tearing pages out of Otto's notebook. Because users have also unlearned the skill the platform took over, they end up worse off than before they began using it: *"the user has then been made more dependent on a worse product!"*

Third, the decay harms attention, and with it moral character (Vallor, Gardiner): someone constantly distracted by ragebait and cat videos may scroll past a friend's cry for help.

They find the remedies on offer (Doctorow's, Zuboff's, O'Reilly et al.'s) a fine start but insufficient, reject simply not using the platforms (*"a world we know is possible because, until a few years ago, we lived in it"*), and call for ethical design, *"such that platforms are remade to not be hostile, exploitative, or morally deskilling"*, alongside breaking up monopolies or running platforms as public utilities.
