## 1. Involuntary Celibates' Experiences of and Grievance over Sexual Exclusion and the Potential Threat of Violence Among Those Active in an Online Incel Forum

**Author:** Anne Speckhard, Molly Ellenberg, Jesse Morton & Alexander Ash
**Year:** 2021
**Type:** Academic article, survey
**Publisher:** *Journal of Strategic Security*, 14(2), 89–121
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.5038/1944-0472.14.2.1910 (open access)

### What this source contributes

The largest primary-data study of incels at the time: over 250 self-identified members of an incel forum, questioned directly rather than read from their posts. The majority reject violence; those who describe themselves as staunch misogynists are the ones likely to endorse it, and forum participation makes that group more misogynistic by validating its views. Respondents report psychological symptoms at high rates and are reluctant to seek professional help. The figures the entry uses in the Appeal (64% depressive symptoms, 60% anxiety, 48% suicidal ideation, one in four screening positive for autistic traits) come from this survey, as summarised in De Vettor et al. (2025).

## 2. Predicting Harm Among Incels (Involuntary Celibates): The Roles of Mental Health, Ideological Belief and Social Networking

**Author:** Joe Whittaker, William Costello & Andrew G. Thomas
**Year:** 2024
**Type:** Government report
**Publisher:** Commission for Countering Extremism, UK Government
**URL:** https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/predicting-harm-among-incels-involuntary-celibates/predicting-harm-among-incels-involuntary-celibates-the-roles-of-mental-health-ideological-belief-and-social-networking-accessible

### What this source contributes

A survey of 561 incels in the UK and the US, with pathway analysis of what predicts a composite measure of harm (angry rumination, revenge planning, hostile sexism, rape myth acceptance, justification of violence). Mental health and ideological belief predict harm more than twice as strongly as social networking, and the two reinforce each other over time. Three quarters of respondents consider themselves part of the incel community, with a median membership of 41 months; only about two thirds agree that an incel ideology exists at all. Roughly a quarter say violence against those who caused them harm is sometimes or often justified.

- [[Incel]] — the source for the claim in Why This Matters that mental health and ideology make each other worse
- [[Blackpill]] — the 80/20 rule, endorsed by about 64% of respondents, is the black pill in survey form

## 3. "I Think Most of Society Hates Us": A Qualitative Thematic Analysis of Interviews with Incels

**Author:** Sarah E. Daly & Shon M. Reed
**Year:** 2022 (online 2021)
**Type:** Academic article, interviews
**Publisher:** *Sex Roles*, 86, 14–33
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-021-01250-5 (paywalled)

### What this source contributes

Interview-based research with men who identify as incels. The title is the theme: the experience of being held in contempt by the outside world, and what that does to the name they give themselves. De Vettor et al. (2025) cite it for a finding the entry relies on at its close: some incels attribute their depression and loneliness, in retrospect, to belonging to the community itself. Not read in full for this entry; used through De Vettor's citation and the published abstract.

## 4. Weaponized Subordination: How Incels Discredit Themselves to Degrade Women

**Author:** Michael Halpin
**Year:** 2022
**Type:** Academic article, theory
**Publisher:** *Gender & Society*, 36(6), 813–837
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/08912432221128545

### What this source contributes

The origin of the concept that carries the entry's Friction. Halpin argues that incels do not fit either side of masculinities theory cleanly: they claim a subordinate position, and then use that position to legitimise the degradation of women. Subordination as a weapon. De Vettor et al. (2025) apply the concept to their forum material, and the entry cites it through them. Not read in full for this entry.

## 5. Psychopathology of Incel (Involuntary Celibate): The Predictive Role of Depression, Paranoia, and Fearful Attachment Style

**Author:** Giacomo Ciocca, Jessica Martinelli, Erika Limoncin, Andrea Sansone, Emmanuele A. Jannini & Lilybeth Fontanesi
**Year:** 2022
**Type:** Conference abstract
**Publisher:** *The Journal of Sexual Medicine*, 19 (supplement)
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsxm.2022.08.021

### What this source contributes

The Incel Traits Scale applied to 770 incels, finding paranoid thinking, anxiety and depression common, along with a fearful attachment style: a negative view of oneself and of others at once, with fear of intimacy and social avoidance. Reported here as summarised in De Vettor et al. (2025); the abstract itself is brief.

## 7. Alphas, Betas, and Incels: Theorizing the Masculinities of the Manosphere

**Author:** Debbie Ging
**Year:** 2019 (online 2017)
**Type:** Academic article, theory
**Publisher:** *Men and Masculinities*, 22(4), 638–657
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1097184X17706401

### What this source contributes

The standard theoretical frame for the manosphere as a set of related communities with a shared market logic of masculinity. Places incels among alphas, betas and the rest, and reads their position as a hybrid masculinity: subordinate in status, dominant in practice. Also a primary source elsewhere in the vault (Looksmaxxing, Sigma Male).
