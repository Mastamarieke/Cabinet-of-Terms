***She recommends a product. You do not know she was paid to. Neither label is visible.***

**Literal meaning:** An **influencer** is a person who has built a social media audience large enough to affect the purchasing decisions, opinions, or behaviours of followers — and who monetises that audience through brand partnerships, sponsored content, and affiliate arrangements. The term replaced earlier vocabulary: celebrity endorsement, word-of-mouth, brand ambassador.

**Origin:** The term entered marketing and journalism from around 2015–2016, as platforms built monetisation infrastructure that made large-audience content creation commercially viable. The shift from "blogger" or "YouTuber" to "**influencer**" was not neutral: it reframed the person as a mechanism of commercial impact rather than a content creator. By 2020, "**influencer**" was standard in marketing, law, and public policy — including regulatory frameworks for advertising disclosure.

The fashion press shows how the status changed. When bloggers began taking seats in the front row of the shows, next to editors taking notes with pen and paper, Suzy Menkes called them "wannabes" and "poseurs" in *The New York Times* (2013). After Milan Fashion Week in 2016, *Vogue*'s online editors dismissed them as pathetic and embarrassing, and Sally Singer called them "the death of style". The objection was their closeness to the brands. The bloggers answered that the magazines depended on the same fashion houses for advertising, loaned clothes and access to the shows. Within a few years *Vogue* was putting influencers on its covers and sending Emma Chamberlain, who started out vlogging her daily life on YouTube in 2017, to interview the guests at the Met Gala. The advertising budgets had moved from the magazines to the people the brands now called influencers.

> A person whose social media audience is large enough to monetise — where the relationship with followers is the commercial asset.

**The Appeal:** The **influencer** economy has democratised access to audience and income in ways that legacy media never did. People from outside traditional media centres, from marginalised communities, and with niche expertise have built substantial platforms and incomes. The authenticity claim — I'm a real person sharing what I actually use — has genuine basis in many cases.

**The Friction:** The term "influence" conceals the commercial architecture. [[Highlight Reel]] — showing only the best — is the default format. [[Curated Life]] — life as editorial product — is the mode. The follower relationship feels personal; it is structured. [[Comparison Culture]] runs on **influencer** content: the visible life sets a standard that followers measure themselves against, without seeing the conditions, contracts, or editing behind it. [[Mimetic Desire]] is the mechanism: the influencer functions as a model who shows followers what is worth wanting — and the platform is designed to keep refreshing that model. Disclosure requirements exist but are inconsistently applied and widely gamed.

The front row at a fashion show makes the difference concrete. A fashion journalist attends on invitation, is not paid by the fashion house, and can write a negative review of a collection that disappoints. Large influencers are sometimes paid to attend; smaller ones get their flight, hotel and outfit in exchange for a story, a post or a reel, with the content often agreed in advance and the angle positive. What the brand pays for is the [[Parasocial Relationship]]: the follower feels they know the influencer personally, and a product recommended by someone who feels like a friend sells better than an advertisement in a magazine. Some creators now do the critic's work as well. Mandy Lee worked as a data analyst in the tech and cosmetics industries and now uses data analysis to review and forecast fashion trends on Instagram, while also being paid by Uniqlo and Burberry, so the review and the advertisement come from the same account.

The influence does not stay on the screen. Fabel Friet, a chip shop in Amsterdam's Nine Streets, drew little attention after it opened in 2020. From around 2023, videos on TikTok and Instagram of its fries with parmesan and truffle mayonnaise brought queues that at times reached the bridge over the Keizersgracht, full of people filming their portion. Dutch has a word for it by now, the *TikTokrij* (TikTok queue), and Fabel Friet is not the only one in the neighbourhood: Chun Café in the Berenstraat and the biscuit shop Het Koekemannetje, also in the Runstraat, have them too. Residents took the municipality to court over the shop's operating licence, with the first hearing in September 2026. The owners empty the bins in the street 24 times a day and employ crowd managers at weekends, and say their queue has become a symbol of overtourism. No influencer contract is involved: the customers in the queue make the videos that bring the next customers.

**Why This Matters:** "**Influencer**" is a word that makes a commercial relationship sound like a friendship. Once you know that, the sponsored post is a different kind of sentence.
