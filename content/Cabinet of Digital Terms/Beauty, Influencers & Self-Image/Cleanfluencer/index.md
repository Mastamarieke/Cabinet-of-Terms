***You know her pantry better than your own, and watching her refill it made you calm. What did that calm cost you, and who gained from it?***

**Literal meaning:** *Cleanfluencer* is a blend of *clean*, free of dirt and in order, and *influencer* ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/cleanfluencer "Read the Wiktionary entry for cleanfluencer.")). An [[Influencer|influencer]] is someone whose followers buy what she uses. Put together, a **[[Cleanfluencer|cleanfluencer]]** cleans, tidies and refills her home on camera, and persuades her audience to buy the products in view. What emerges sits in neither word: housework, the work that was always done out of sight and without pay, becomes something to watch and something to earn from.

**Origin:** ==The Guardian put the word in quotation marks in January 2019== ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#1. Meet the 'cleanfluencers', the online gurus who like things nice and tidy|Mahdawi, 2019]]: a feature on cleaning accounts on Instagram), a month after ==Marie Kondo==[^kondo] began tidying American homes on Netflix. The best known was ==Mrs Hinch==[^hinch], a hairdresser from Essex who called cleaning *hinching* and her followers the Hinch Army. ==When she praised a cleaning cloth, it sold out for weeks and its maker's website crashed== ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#1. Meet the 'cleanfluencers', the online gurus who like things nice and tidy|Mahdawi, 2019]]: the manufacturer confirmed the rise in sales). On TikTok the genre became #CleanTok, with the *restock*: a fridge, pantry or bathroom cabinet refilled with products taken out of their packaging and lined up in clear containers.

[^kondo]: Japanese tidying consultant, known for her book *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up* (in English since 2014). Her series *Tidying Up with Marie Kondo* started on Netflix on 1 January 2019, and charity shops reported a rise in donations. [Netflix: Tidying Up with Marie Kondo](https://www.netflix.com/title/80209379)

[^hinch]: Sophie Hinchliffe, on Instagram since March 2018. By 2021 she had 4.1 million followers, deals with Procter & Gamble and her own range at Tesco and Asda. [Casey & Littler, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/00380261211059591)

> Housework filmed as a calming routine: the cleanfluencer, platforms and shops earn from it, the viewers, mostly women, buy the dream and still clean unpaid.

**The Appeal:** The videos bring order to a messy life. They repeat, the before and after always works, and the sound is turned up for ==ASMR==[^asmr]: lids click, containers fill, labels roll out of the printer. ==Mrs Hinch says folding is so therapeutic, and her book promises to soothe the soul== ([[Casey-Cleanfluencer-2022|Casey & Littler, 2022]]: a study of her posts and books as cleaning sold as therapy). For people who never learned to clean at home, the **[[Cleanfluencer|cleanfluencer]]** is also a teacher, as Jo Littler points out ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#2. Emma Casey and Jo Littler in conversation: How social media cleanfluencers are refashioning the domestic narrative|Casey & Littler, 2023]]: a conversation between the two researchers). Viewers come back to the same voice and the same kitchen, and the bond feels mutual although it runs one way, the [[Parasocial Relationship]] that makes her recommendation sound like a friend's. The calm travels: the American creator ==Operation Niki==[^niki] even cleans her hotel room again.

[^asmr]: Autonomous sensory meridian response: the tingling, calm feeling some people get from soft, repeated sounds such as whispering, tapping or pouring. Videos made to cause it are a genre of their own. [Wikipedia: ASMR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASMR)

[^niki]: A YouTube channel with 1.4 million subscribers since 2022, described as *Jesus, Mom, Wife, Cook, Clean, Organize, Self Care*, with Amazon links under every video. In a series tagged #hotelcleaning, from 2023 on, she cleans hotel rooms with, as one title says, great respect for housekeeping. One of her most watched clips, 4.4 million views, is a clean-with-me in a hospital room. [YouTube: Operation Niki](https://www.youtube.com/@operationniki)

**The Friction:** You paid through the links under the video, and the shops behind them earned on every container, gadget, cleaning product and decoration. She is paid for performing the housework that, for most of the women watching, stays unpaid. ==Restock videos sell both the products being organised and the bins that organise them== ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#3. Unhinged consumerism or aspirational organisation? A critical discourse analysis of restock videos|Riboni & Ringrow, 2025]]: an analysis of Instagram restock posts, mostly by wives and mothers). ==Cleanfluencers drive overconsumption by taking products out of their own packaging and decanting them into new, prettier plastic containers== ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#5. As social media creators push overconsumption, some question whether 'eco' influencing is possible|Mather, 2024]]: Yahoo News on influencers and overconsumption), and [[Deinfluencing]], creators telling followers what not to buy, does not change that, because not selling runs against the job itself. In product overload videos, filmed for the colours and the sound, creators pour one cleaner after another into the sink or the toilet: ==by one wastewater campaigner's estimate more in a single video than many households use in six months, and mixed cleaners can be lethal when breathed in== ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#6. TikTok Cleaning 'Product Overload' Trend Could Be Dangerous: Experts|Head, 2022]]: Business Insider, with a wastewater campaigner and a pharmacist). [[Casey-Cleanfluencer-2022|Casey & Littler (2022)]] see an old script in new packaging: cleaning as therapy keeps women willing to do the housework, while the dirty work disappears with a snap of the fingers and *Mr Hinch very rarely appears with a mop in his hand*.

**Why This Matters:** The next time a tidy cupboard on your screen makes you feel calm, ask what had to be bought for it, and who cleans when the camera is off. The same pull can go the other way: ==a review of 52 studies found that influencers can make their followers buy and live more sustainably, especially when they are credible, know their subject and live green themselves== ([[Secondary-Cleanfluencer#7. The impact of influencers on sustainable consumption: A systematic literature review|Munaro et al., 2024]]: a systematic review in Sustainable Production and Consumption).
