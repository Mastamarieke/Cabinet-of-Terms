Kim, Kim, E, and Shoenberger (2023) test the effectiveness of virtual influencer endorsements by comparing **human-like virtual influencers** (HVIs) against **anime-like virtual influencers** (AVIs). Their theoretical frame is the **CASA paradigm** — *Computers Are Social Actors* — which holds that people automatically apply social rules to computer-generated agents that appear sufficiently human-like. The more human the appearance, the more the agent triggers automatic social trust responses, increasing perceived social presence and message credibility.

Their key finding: HVI endorsements produce greater perceived message credibility than AVI endorsements — but only when sponsorship is not disclosed. Once AI origin is made explicit, the trust advantage disappears entirely. Disclosure is the critical interruption point — and its burial in fine print is therefore not an oversight but a mechanism.

**Analytical function in the Virtual Influencer entry:** Provides the CASA paradigm as the theoretical explanation for why anthropomorphic design functions as trust engineering. The disclosure finding anchors the Friction section: the advantage exists precisely because disclosure is suppressed.
