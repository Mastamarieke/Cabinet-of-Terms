***Your downloads folder has 4,000 files. You have not opened most of them. You have never once decided to keep them.***

**Literal meaning**
*Digital* — existing in digital form. *Hoarding* — accumulation beyond use. **Digital Hoarding** is the persistent accumulation of digital files, emails, screenshots and media together with an unwillingness to delete them. Accumulation is the default state rather than the symptom: deletion is a decision, and nothing in a folder ever requires you to make it. Difficulty letting go is the escalation that turns a common condition into a distressing one, not the thing that defines it.

**Origin**
Nick Neave and colleagues at Northumbria University first documented **digital hoarding** as a distinct behavioural pattern in 2016. They tested it against the clinical literature on hoarding disorder rather than assuming the fit: their 2019 study found that only OCD and physical-hoarding scores predicted digital hoarding, and concluded that it shares *some* features of physical hoarding. The standard definition in the field since then speaks of unwillingness to delete, not inability. Unlike physical hoarding, **digital hoarding** is enabled by near-unlimited cheap storage — there is no material pressure to delete.

> The persistent accumulation of digital files nobody is ever required to review — cheap storage removes the moment of decision, and for some people what began as never getting round to it becomes an unwillingness to let go.

**The Appeal**
Keeping files feels rational: storage is cheap, you might need it later, and deletion is irreversible. The logic of abundance makes accumulation the path of least resistance.

**The Friction**
Neave et al. (2018) document that **digital hoarding** produces anxiety, cognitive overload, and impaired information retrieval — the same consequences as physical hoarding, without the visible signal. Later work locates the mechanism in depletion rather than compulsion: Liu & Liu (2024) find fatigue mediating between accumulation and cognitive failures across 801 participants, and research on university students identifies decision fatigue alongside fear of missing out and emotional attachment as predictors. [[Technostress]] is the clinical name for that anxiety and overload: digital hoarding is one of its accumulation-specific triggers, alongside notification overload and constant connectivity. [[Digital Overload]] compounds it from a different angle — it is not only that files exceed the capacity to process, but that an unopened folder never forces the review a physical pile eventually would, so the overload accumulates silently instead of announcing itself. Platform design accelerates both: unlimited cloud storage removes the friction that would otherwise trigger sorting and deletion. [[Surveillance Capitalism]] shapes the incentive on the platform's side of that design choice: storage that never fills is not generosity, it keeps data resident on company infrastructure indefinitely, available for analysis, rather than encouraging the deletion that would put it out of reach. None of it is visible until it disrupts work or causes distress, which is why the condition is usually named only at the point where it has already become one.

**Why This Matters**
Once you see **digital hoarding** as a design outcome rather than a personal failure, you see that the architecture of unlimited storage and easy accumulation produces the condition — and that the solution is not willpower but friction by design.
